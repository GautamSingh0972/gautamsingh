When Investing Starts to Look Like Gambling

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Based on Planet Money's reporting on margin trading, sports betting and leveraged investing

After listening to Planet Money's episodes on margin trading, sports betting and leveraged investing, I was left with a few questions. The episodes show how borrowing money can make risky decisions much more dangerous. But they leave a bigger question unanswered: when does financial risk stop being an individual choice and become a problem for the wider system?

First, what happens when you invest with borrowed money?

Margin trading is simple in theory. You buy a stock using some of your own money and some money borrowed from a broker.

Imagine you have £100 and borrow another £100. If the stock rises by 10%, you make £20 — a 20% return on your own money. But if it falls by 10%, you lose £20, or 20% of your original money.

The problem becomes much worse during a crash.

Research on the Indian stock market found that stocks eligible for margin trading tended to move together more during severe market downturns. More than a quarter of the increase in this comovement could be explained by margin trading.

The important point is that this does not mean margin stocks fell 27% more. The 27% figure refers to the increase in how closely stocks moved together.

Why does this happen?

Prices fall → margin calls → investors are forced to sell → prices fall further

Leverage can therefore turn an ordinary fall into a feedback loop.

But were margin stocks already different?

There is an obvious problem with this argument.

Margin-eligible stocks were not randomly chosen. They tended to be larger, more liquid and more actively traded. Maybe these stocks were always more likely to move together during a crisis.

The researchers dealt with this by looking at stocks around the eligibility threshold. This allowed them to compare stocks that were very similar except for whether they qualified for margin trading.

The result still showed a stronger increase in comovement during severe downturns for margin-eligible stocks.

So the evidence suggests that leverage itself matters, rather than the result being entirely explained by the type of companies being traded.

Then came South Korea

South Korea provides a different version of the same story.

The country's stock market had experienced a huge rally, particularly in semiconductor and AI-related companies. Expectations had become extremely high.

Then those expectations changed.

A company can report strong profits and still see its share price fall if investors expected something even better. The problem is not necessarily that the company suddenly became bad. It is that the price had already assumed a very good future.

This creates a possible sequence:

Huge rally → very high expectations → expectations change → prices fall → leveraged investors lose → forced selling → larger fall

This suggests that leverage may have been an accelerator rather than the original spark.

What happens to the people who are forced to sell?

This creates another uncomfortable problem.

Imagine you own a stock worth $100. A crash pushes it down to $60 and your broker forces you to sell.

A few months later, the stock returns to $100.

The market has recovered.

But you have not.

You sold at $60, so you no longer own the stock when it returns to $100.

This is one of the biggest differences between an ordinary investor and a highly leveraged investor. Leverage can force you out of the market at exactly the moment when prices are falling.

South Korean regulators have responded by placing restrictions on some leveraged investment products, including restrictions on new single-stock leveraged products and higher minimum cash requirements.

But how much of the crash was actually caused by forced selling?

This is where the evidence becomes much less clear.

There is no simple way to say that, for example, 20% of the fall was caused by forced selling and the remaining 80% was a normal correction.

Several things were happening at the same time:

  • Investors were taking profits after a huge rally.
  • Expectations about future earnings were changing.
  • Ordinary investors were selling.
  • Leveraged investors were being forced to sell.
  • Leveraged products could add further selling pressure.
  • Falling prices could create even more selling.

So leverage probably made the decline worse, but the exact contribution of forced selling is difficult to measure.

If leveraged ETFs are investments, why do they sometimes feel like bets?

Technically, a leveraged ETF is an investment product.

But the way people use it can make it look very different.

Someone who buys a normal diversified ETF and holds it for ten years is clearly investing.

Someone who puts all their savings into a 3x ETF because they think a stock will rise tomorrow is doing something very different.

The product is still an investment product. The behaviour starts to look more like a bet.

There is therefore a spectrum:

Long-term investing → short-term speculation → highly leveraged trading → gambling-like behaviour

The boundary is not always obvious.

And are the same people doing both?

This is perhaps the most interesting question.

There is evidence that some people who participate heavily in gambling also engage in high-risk financial trading. Both activities can involve quick decisions, frequent transactions, large potential losses and the temptation to chase losses.

But that does not mean that young sports bettors are generally the same people buying leveraged ETFs or trading on margin.

The evidence is better at showing overlap between some groups than proving that one activity causes people to start doing the other.

The common factor may simply be a preference for high-risk, short-term decisions.

So why should the government care?

Adults are allowed to make risky financial decisions all the time.

People can buy shares, borrow money, trade options or bet on football.

So where should the government draw the line?

The strongest arguments for intervention usually appear when there is more than just individual risk.

Addiction matters. So does misleading information, easy access to credit, aggressive marketing and financial products designed to make repeated risk-taking extremely easy.

There is also a difference between losing money yourself and creating a feedback loop that affects other investors.

That makes the debate less about "Should adults be allowed to take risks?" and more about "How much friction should exist before someone can repeatedly take those risks?"

Colorado's experiment

Colorado is taking this approach with sports betting.

It has not banned betting. Instead, it has introduced restrictions designed to make repeated betting harder.

One example is a limit of six separate deposits that a sportsbook can accept from an individual during a gaming day. Credit-card deposits are also banned, and there are restrictions on certain forms of marketing.

But there are obvious ways around some of these restrictions.

Someone could potentially use another sportsbook. They could also borrow money elsewhere and deposit that money into their bank account before betting.

So the law targets how money enters the sportsbook, rather than eliminating access to borrowed money altogether.

Why six deposits?

The number six sounds very precise.

But there is no obvious economic formula that says six deposits is the point at which gambling becomes dangerous.

It is better understood as a policy threshold.

The idea is to add friction to a cycle like:

Deposit → lose → deposit again → lose → deposit again

Even a small amount of friction could matter if it gives someone time to stop.

Whether it actually works is another question.

How will Colorado know whether it worked?

Colorado will collect data from sportsbooks and publish reports on gambling activity. But this will not be a perfect experiment.

People can change sportsbooks, change payment methods or move their betting activity elsewhere.

So even if betting changes after the law, it may be difficult to know exactly how much of that change was caused by the policy.

The bigger connection

Margin trading and sports betting are obviously not the same thing.

One involves owning financial assets. The other involves betting on an uncertain outcome.

But technology has made both activities faster, easier and more accessible.

A person can borrow money, buy a leveraged ETF and lose a large amount of money in minutes. Someone else can deposit money into a sportsbook and place another bet just seconds after losing the previous one.

In both cases, the problem is not simply risk.

It is the combination of leverage, speed, easy access and repeated decisions.

That may be the real lesson behind the Planet Money episodes.

The question is no longer simply whether people should be allowed to take financial risks. It is whether the system should make those risks easy enough to take that people can lose control before they have time to reconsider.

The takeaway

Leverage does not create every crash, and gambling restrictions cannot eliminate every risky behaviour.

But both examples show how small individual decisions can become larger feedback loops.

A margin call can turn a falling price into more selling. A losing bet can lead to another deposit and an even larger bet.

The difficult part is deciding where individual freedom ends and financial protection begins.

And perhaps that is the most interesting question that Planet Money leaves behind.
