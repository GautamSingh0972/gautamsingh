Why Are Used Cars Still So Expensive?

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Follow-up to Planet Money's "Why are used cars priced like new cars?"

The used-car market has gone through one of the strangest price shocks in recent economic history. During the pandemic, a shortage of new cars pushed buyers into the used market. But the story did not end when factories reopened. New cars became more expensive, manufacturers moved away from cheap sedans, and a missing generation of pandemic-era vehicles is still moving through the used-car market.

Planet Money's episode explains these forces, but it leaves several important questions unanswered. How much does each force actually matter? When will the pandemic shortage disappear? Why have cheap cars become so hard to find? And does the same story apply in Britain?

Here is what the evidence tells us.

1. How much of the 5.5% rise comes from each cause?

The first thing to understand is that the 5.5% figure is an observed increase in used-car prices, not a decomposition of the market into three separate percentages.

The episode identifies three forces:

  • The price waterfall: higher new-car prices push up the prices of comparable used cars.
  • The pandemic supply hole: fewer cars were produced during 2020–21, so fewer cars are now entering the used market at the normal rate.
  • The disappearance of cheap sedans: manufacturers have shifted production toward SUVs, crossovers and other higher-priced vehicles.

There is no reliable number saying that, for example, 40% of the increase came from the first cause, 35% from the second and 25% from the third. The reason is that the effects overlap. A shortage of new cars can raise new-car prices, which then pushes more consumers toward used cars. One cause can therefore amplify another.

Answer: We cannot sensibly divide the 5.5% rise into three independent pieces using the information in the episode. The important conclusion is that the three forces reinforce each other rather than operate separately.

2. When will the pandemic "snake" finish digesting the mouse?

The pandemic created a hole in the normal supply of cars. Production fell sharply in 2020 and remained disrupted into 2021. Cars normally enter the used market gradually as they age, so that missing production does not disappear immediately. It moves through the age distribution of used cars.

By 2026, vehicles produced in 2020 are roughly six years old and those produced in 2021 are roughly five years old. The market is therefore already moving through the missing batch.

The most visible effects should continue fading through roughly 2026–28. But that does not mean used-car prices will suddenly collapse when the last missing cars arrive.

The reason is that the pandemic shortage is only one part of the story. New vehicles are still much more expensive than they were before the pandemic, and the supply of inexpensive cars has changed. Once the pandemic shortage is gone, those structural forces can continue supporting used-car prices.

Answer: The pandemic "mouse" is being digested gradually rather than on one exact date. Its strongest effects should fade through the late 2020s, but there is no strong reason to expect a sudden used-car price crash when that happens.

3. Are cheap sedans gone temporarily or permanently?

The decline of cheap sedans looks more structural than temporary. American consumers have increasingly preferred crossovers, SUVs and pickups, and manufacturers have responded by putting more of their investment and production capacity into those vehicles.

There is also a financial incentive. A manufacturer has limited factory capacity, and using that capacity for a higher-priced vehicle can be more attractive than using it for a small, inexpensive sedan. Once consumers move away from sedans, fewer sedans are produced; once fewer are produced, consumers have fewer choices.

This creates a feedback loop:

Fewer cheap cars → fewer choices → more consumers buy larger vehicles → even less incentive to produce cheap cars.

Could Chinese EVs fill the gap?

In theory, yes. Chinese manufacturers have become highly competitive in producing relatively inexpensive electric vehicles. If those vehicles could enter the American market at low prices, they could put pressure on manufacturers to compete in the budget segment.

In practice, U.S. trade barriers make large-scale Chinese EV entry difficult. That means the possibility exists economically, but it is not currently a major source of inexpensive cars for American consumers.

Answer: The disappearance of cheap sedans appears to be a structural change rather than just a pandemic pause. Cheap Chinese EVs could theoretically refill part of the budget segment, but trade barriers currently limit that possibility.

4. How much of new-car tariffs gets passed on to used cars?

A tariff on a new imported car is not directly charged to someone buying a used car. The important effect is indirect.

Imagine a tariff makes new cars more expensive. Some consumers who would have bought new cars now decide to buy used. That increases demand for used vehicles. If the supply of used cars cannot increase quickly, their prices rise.

The chain is therefore:

Tariff → higher new-car prices → fewer new-car purchases → more used-car demand → higher used-car prices.

Economic modelling supports this substitution effect, but there is no single percentage that can be applied to every used car. The effect depends on the vehicle segment, the size of the tariff, how easily buyers can substitute between new and used vehicles, and how much used-car supply is available.

Answer: There is no reliable single pass-through percentage from new-car tariffs to used-car prices. The used-car effect is indirect and depends on supply and demand conditions.

5. Should CPI count the cost of borrowing for a car?

This is really a question about what an inflation index is supposed to measure.

Suppose two people buy the same £30,000 car. One pays cash. The other borrows the money at a high interest rate. The second person's total financial cost is much higher. From the perspective of household affordability, ignoring interest seems strange.

But there is a problem with putting that interest directly into CPI. Interest is the price of financing the purchase, not the price of the car itself. If interest rates rise while the car's sticker price remains unchanged, including finance costs could make measured inflation rise even though the underlying price of the car has not changed.

This is why the distinction between a price index and a cost-of-living measure matters. A price index can reasonably exclude financing while a separate household affordability measure could include it.

Answer: There are good arguments on both sides. Keeping borrowing costs out of CPI makes sense if CPI is measuring the prices of goods and services. But publishing a separate measure of the cost of buying a car with financing would give consumers a much better picture of affordability.

6. Are used EVs really underpriced?

Used EVs have depreciated rapidly, but a low price does not automatically mean that the market is mispricing them.

There are real reasons for faster depreciation:

  • Technology changes quickly. A newer EV may offer much more range, faster charging and better software.
  • Battery uncertainty matters. Buyers cannot observe battery health as easily as they can observe the condition of many conventional components.
  • New EV prices affect old EV prices. When manufacturers cut the price of a new model, used versions of the same model generally have to become cheaper too.

But some of the fears around used EVs may also be excessive. Recent UK evidence from 47.4 million MOT tests found that high-mileage EVs had lower MOT failure rates than petrol cars at 90,000–120,000 miles and above. MOT tests do not measure battery health, however, so the finding should not be interpreted as proof that every used EV has a healthy battery.

The UK market is also showing signs that the extreme depreciation of recent years may be easing. In 2026, used-EV prices have begun to stabilise or rise depending on the dataset, while transactions have increased sharply.

Answer: Used EVs are not necessarily "underpriced." Their low prices reflect real uncertainty about technology, batteries and future resale values. At the same time, falling prices may create genuine bargains if consumers are overestimating those risks.

7. Does the UK used-car market show the same pattern?

Yes, but the British version looks different because Britain buys a different mix of cars.

The basic pandemic mechanism was similar. Fewer new cars were produced, new-car supply was restricted, and consumers who could not or did not want to wait for a new vehicle moved into the used market. That pushed used prices higher.

But Britain is much more dependent on small cars. Superminis make up roughly a third of UK used-car transactions. That means Britain's affordability problem is less about the disappearance of cheap sedans and more about the availability and price of small hatchbacks and other inexpensive cars.

The UK is also seeing a rapid increase in used EV transactions. In the second quarter of 2026, battery-electric used-car transactions rose 67% year-on-year and reached a record 5.5% share of the used market, according to the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders.

So the UK provides an interesting comparison. The pandemic supply shock was similar, but the vehicle mix means the consequences are different.

Answer: The UK shows the same broad supply-and-demand pattern, but not exactly the same product story. Small hatchbacks and superminis matter much more in Britain than cheap sedans do in America.

The bigger picture

The most important lesson from the Planet Money episode is that the used-car problem is no longer just a temporary pandemic story.

The pandemic created the original shortage, but several longer-term changes followed it. New cars became more expensive. Manufacturers shifted toward larger and more expensive vehicles. Consumers changed what they bought. And the missing production from 2020–21 continued moving through the used-car market years after the factories reopened.

That is why simply waiting for the pandemic shortage to disappear is unlikely to return the market to its 2019 structure.

The deeper economic question is not just "Why are used cars expensive?" It is: what happens when an industry stops producing the inexpensive products that used to form the bottom of the market?

The answer may depend on whether existing manufacturers start producing cheaper vehicles again, whether new competitors enter the market, and whether electric vehicles eventually become the affordable used-car option that small petrol cars once were.

Key Terms

  • Price waterfall — the tendency for higher new-car prices to feed into higher prices for comparable used cars.
  • Supply shock — a sudden change in the availability of a good that changes its market price.
  • Substitution effect — when consumers switch from one product to another because the relative prices or availability change.
  • Depreciation — the fall in an asset's value as it ages.
  • Residual value — what a vehicle is expected to be worth after a period of ownership.
  • Pass-through — the extent to which a change in one price, such as a tariff, is reflected in another price paid by consumers.
  • CPI — the Consumer Price Index, a measure designed to track changes in the prices consumers pay for a basket of goods and services.
