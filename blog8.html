Cheaper Oil, Costlier Money: Can the World Economy Grow Through 2027?

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Based on Goldman Sachs Research’s 2027 global economic outlook

Goldman Sachs expects the global economy to speed up slightly next year, from 2.7% growth to 2.8%. The headline barely moves. What sits underneath it is more interesting: falling oil prices and an AI spending boom pulling one way, tight monetary policy and fading government stimulus pulling the other.

Goldman Sachs Research, led by chief economist Jan Hatzius, published its 2027 outlook on 13 November 2026. Its global growth forecast of 2.8% is exactly in line with consensus, so on the surface there is nothing controversial about it.

But a global average can hide a lot. The forecast is really a balance of four forces, and they hit different countries in very different ways. The question is not just how fast the world grows, but which economies benefit and how fragile that balance is.

1. Why does cheaper oil matter so much?

The title of the report gives away its biggest theme. Energy was a drag on growth in 2026. Goldman’s commodity strategists expect that to reverse, with Brent crude falling to around $78 a barrel by the end of 2027 and liquefied natural gas prices normalising in a similar way.

Cheaper energy helps an economy through two channels. Households and businesses spend less on fuel and heating, leaving more money for everything else. And lower energy prices pull headline inflation down, which gives central banks room to stop raising interest rates.

Oil falls → inflation eases → central banks stop hiking → spending recovers

The catch is timing. Energy costs reach consumers with a lag, especially in Europe, where wholesale gas prices feed into household bills slowly. So Europe will keep feeling this winter’s high prices well into next year, even if oil futures are already falling.

The limit: Energy shifts from a headwind to only a modest boost in 2027. A falling oil price helps, but it does not undo the damage from a year of high bills overnight.

2. Is AI really driving growth?

The second big driver is AI investment. Goldman expects global spending on AI to rise from $1.1 trillion in 2026 to $1.8 trillion in 2027. Most of that increase, more than $500 billion, comes from extra US spending on hardware such as chips, servers and data centres.

Measure Goldman estimate What it tells us
Global AI investment, 2026 $1.1 trillion The starting level of spending
Global AI investment, 2027 $1.8 trillion A rise of roughly 64% in one year
Boost to global growth 0.4 percentage points Around a seventh of the 2.8% forecast
Boost to US growth 0.6 percentage points After correcting for undercounted chip investment

There is a subtle point here that is easy to miss. GDP growth depends on the change in investment, not its level. If AI spending is huge but stops increasing, its contribution to growth falls towards zero.

Contribution to growth ≈ change in investment ÷ size of the economy

Goldman expects the year on year increase in AI investment, as a share of GDP, to peak in the first half of 2027. After that, spending could stay enormous and still add less to growth each quarter. Anyone treating AI as a permanent growth booster should keep that in mind.

Goldman describes the AI transition in two phases:

  • The build-out: heavy investment in hardware, limited productivity gains and little effect on jobs. This is where we are now.
  • The reorganisation: businesses redesign how they work, productivity rises and the labour market is disrupted. Goldman expects these effects to become more noticeable in 2028 and 2029.

3. Who wins and who falls behind?

The regional forecasts are where Goldman takes a view against the market.

Economy Goldman vs consensus Main reason
United States Above Resilient investment, especially in AI
United Kingdom Above Growth holds up despite continuing fiscal drag
Japan Above Tax cuts, defence and investment add about 0.7 points
China Slightly above (4.5% vs 4.4%) Strong exports, weak property market and consumers
South Korea and Taiwan Strong AI-related semiconductor exports
Euro area Below Tighter financial conditions and high winter energy costs

China is the most interesting case. Its exports and trade surplus look strong, but spending at home stays weak because of the property slump and cautious consumers. That weakness spreads abroad. Goldman estimates it takes 0.3 points off German growth, 0.2 points off Central Europe and 0.1 points off global growth.

The pattern is clear. Economies that sell machinery and cars to China are struggling, while economies that sell chips to AI companies are booming. In 2027, what a country exports may matter as much as how well its own economy is managed.

4. If inflation is falling, why are rates still rising?

Inflation should ease next year as energy prices fall and the effect of US tariffs drops out of the annual comparison by spring. Goldman forecasts US core PCE inflation, the Federal Reserve’s preferred measure, to fall from 3.2% at the end of 2026 to 2.4% at the end of 2027.

Even so, most major central banks are expected to raise rates a little further before they turn. Central banks look at where inflation is now, not just where it is heading, and inflation is still above target almost everywhere.

Central bank Next moves During 2027 Eventual resting rate
Federal Reserve Hikes in December and March One cut in December 2027 3.25% to 3.5%, after three cuts in 2028
European Central Bank Final hike in December Cuts back towards normal 2.25% by the end of 2027

Goldman describes its own calls as leaning dovish. For the US, it thinks the causes of inflation look temporary and the economy is the most exposed to any slowdown in AI spending, so the next surprise is more likely to be cuts than extra hikes.

The Bank of England forecast stands out for UK households. A peak of 4.25% followed by a full year on hold means mortgage and savings rates could stay higher for longer than many people expect.

5. What could go wrong?

Goldman says the risks to its forecast are tilted to the downside. The main ones are:

  • Geopolitics: renewed escalation in the Iran war could push energy prices up again and remove the forecast’s main tailwind.
  • Government debt: large deficits could push up long term bond yields, with France singled out as a weak spot.
  • An AI disappointment: spending could fall short because of power and capacity limits, regulation, stress in credit markets or weaker demand. Because AI does so much of the work in the US forecast, this risk matters most there.
  • Fading stimulus: the large government spending programmes of recent years are winding down. Goldman sees only a slightly positive fiscal impulse in developed economies, with small boosts in Germany and the US.

Several of these risks are linked. An oil shock would raise inflation, which would keep rates higher, which would tighten credit, which could in turn slow AI investment. The forecast depends on several things going right at the same time.

The takeaway

The 2.8% headline hides a fragile balance. Much of the upside rests on two things happening together: oil falling as forecast and AI investment keeping its pace through the first half of 2027. If either slips, the picture weakens quickly, and the US, which leans hardest on AI spending, has the most to lose.

Three things are worth watching next year. The price of Brent crude against the $78 forecast shows whether the energy tailwind is real. Capital spending plans from the big technology companies give an early signal of whether the AI build-out is slowing. And the language from central banks in the first quarter will show whether the expected final hikes happen or are quietly dropped.

The outlook is not a boom and it is not a bust. It is a world where cheaper energy and AI spending just about offset tight money, and where the details will decide which economies come out ahead.

Key terms

Brent crude: The main international benchmark for the price of oil, measured in dollars per barrel.

Consensus: The average forecast of professional economists, used as a benchmark for whether a forecast is optimistic or pessimistic.

Core PCE inflation: A US measure of price growth that excludes volatile food and energy prices. It is the Federal Reserve’s preferred inflation gauge.

Fiscal impulse: The change in how much government borrowing and spending is adding to, or taking away from, economic growth.

Terminal rate: The level at which a central bank expects its interest rate to settle once a cycle of hikes or cuts is over.

Financial conditions: How easy it is for households and firms to borrow and raise money, reflecting interest rates, bond yields, share prices and the exchange rate.
