The Stolen Beach and the Island With No Deeds: Property Rights in the Caribbean

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Based on Planet Money's Summer School World Tour

One morning in 2008, a beach in Coral Spring, Jamaica, was simply gone. Overnight, thieves had dug up roughly 500 truckloads of pristine white sand and driven it away in convoys of dump trucks. Around 700 kilometres to the east, on the tiny island of Barbuda, land works in a way most economists would call impossible: no rent, no taxes, no deeds. For over a century, any Barbudan could claim a plot, build on it, and pass it on, but never sell it, because the land belongs to all Barbudans in common.

These two stories are really one story: what happens to public and communal property when there is serious money to be made from it. This blog unveils the questions that Planet Money's episode "Sand heists and property rights in the Caribbean" left out, and what the answers reveal about who really controls paradise.

Who actually steals a beach?

Stealing 500 truckloads of sand is not a smash-and-grab; it is a logistics operation. The Coral Spring theft appears to have been organised by a collusion of resort developers, heavy-haul transport operators, and corrupt police. The suspected beneficiaries were rival hotel developers along Jamaica's north coast, who needed high-grade white sand to build or replenish artificial beaches for luxury resorts, because in Caribbean tourism, the beach is the product.

The most telling detail is the alleged police escorts. Moving hundreds of heavy dump trucks along major arterial roads like the A1 highway at night, without a single interception, requires high-level cover. Officers of the Jamaica Constabulary Force reportedly provided route clearance and protection for the convoys.

And then: nothing. Four men, truck drivers and business operators, were charged in 2009, but all charges were dropped in 2010 after key witnesses received death threats and refused to testify. No politician, police commander or hotel owner was ever publicly named or convicted. The beach was never returned.

Where did the sand end up?

Locals say the sand is "long gone", and the physics agrees with them. The stolen sand was almost certainly dumped straight onto degraded or artificial beachfronts at hotels under construction along the north and west coasts. But beaches are not static piles; they sit in high-energy marine environments where waves, longshore currents and storm surges constantly move sand along the coast in littoral cells.

  • Erosion dynamics — unengineered beach nourishment typically washes away within one to five years on coasts with strong littoral drift or hurricane waves.
  • Retention methods — to keep imported sand in place, resorts must build costly coastal engineering structures such as groynes, breakwaters or artificial reefs to trap it.

In other words, a million dollars of stolen sand has likely been redistributed by the sea itself.

Barbuda: the island that ran on trust instead of title

Barbuda's communal system dates back generations: the land is held collectively by the people of Barbuda, and the 2007 Barbuda Land Act wrote that custom into law, requiring major developments to be approved by the Barbuda Council and the community. Then Hurricane Irma flattened the island in 2017, and while residents were evacuated, the central government of Antigua and Barbuda began dismantling the communal system, repealing key parts of the Act and offering Barbudans individual freehold titles to their plots for $1 each.

Prime Minister Gaston Browne's pitch was straight out of the development economics textbook: formal title lets residents mortgage their land, "unlocking capital" for businesses and homes. But the episode never tested that claim.

Would banks actually lend against a $1 title?

For most low-income Barbudans, "unlocking capital" is largely theoretical, because commercial banks do not lend on collateral alone:

  • Debt-service ratios — banks require proof of stable cash flow to service a loan. Without steady employment or income, a land title cannot secure a mortgage, no matter how valuable the plot.
  • Foreclosure risk — if residents do borrow against their titles and default, the bank repossesses the land. Formerly communal land would drain, plot by plot, into private and corporate hands. The mechanism sold as empowerment doubles as a transfer mechanism.
  • Valuation disparities — small residential lots without infrastructure carry low market valuations. The parcels worth real money are the large, contiguous beachfront tracts targeted by resort developers such as Peace Work Ventures and the Ambassador Club, exactly the land the community loses control over once it is individually titled and tradeable.

Was the 2007 Land Act really unconstitutional?

Browne repeatedly claims the communal system "violated the constitution", but the legal reasoning is rarely spelled out. The central government's argument rests on two fronts:

  • Crown title and equal protection — under the 1981 Constitution of Antigua and Barbuda, all public land in the sovereign state belongs to the Crown. Granting Barbudans exclusive communal ownership, the government argues, creates an unconstitutional distinction between citizens born in Antigua and those born in Barbuda.
  • Legislative supremacy — the 2007 Act allegedly restricted Parliament's sovereign right to manage state property and grant leases for national economic development, placing a local council's authority above the central state.

Notice what both arguments have in common: they treat communal ownership as a legal anomaly to be corrected, rather than a century-old institution to be accommodated. That framing is a political choice as much as a legal one.

The courts push back

The legal tide has not run entirely the government's way. In early 2024, the UK Privy Council, the highest court of appeal for Antigua and Barbuda, ruled in favour of Barbudan land rights activists in Mussington & Frank v Development Control Authority, setting two important precedents:

  • Locus standi — individual citizens and communal leaders have direct standing to challenge government-backed environmental decisions and development permits. Ordinary Barbudans can now take the state to court over what happens to their island.
  • Protection for communal tenure — the ruling reinforces judicial protection over customary and communal land systems across the Commonwealth Caribbean, making it harder for central governments to bypass local environmental and land governance laws.

A third way: keep the commons, trade the leases

The Barbuda dispute is usually framed as a binary: communal stagnation versus privatized development. But there is a hybrid model that captures most of the investment incentives without surrendering collective control, and it is widely tested around the world.

The structure is simple. The island commons keep the underlying fee-simple title in perpetuity. Investors and locals instead purchase long-term leasehold estates (50 to 99 years) approved by the Barbuda Council, which can be traded, mortgaged and used as collateral on open markets.

  • Capital incentives — investors get the long-term security needed to finance multi-million dollar projects, and banks get an asset they can actually lend against.
  • Community oversight — lease conditions can enforce environmental protection and local hiring, with automatic reversion to the community if covenants are breached or leases expire.

This is not a thought experiment. Similar regimes operate under First Nations land management in Canada, the iTaukei land trusts that hold most of Fiji, and Bermuda's property leaseholds. The question is not whether the method works; it is whether the central government wants a solution that leaves the commons intact.

The federation that fell apart

Hovering behind both stories is a bigger Caribbean question: why do these small islands face giants alone? They almost didn't. The West Indian Federation (1958–1962) tried to bind the British Caribbean into a single state, and collapsed within four years under three political fights:

  • Jamaica's discontent — Jamaica felt over-represented in financial contributions relative to its parliamentary representation. Norman Manley called a referendum in 1961, and Jamaicans voted to leave.
  • Trinidad's exit — after Jamaica's departure, Premier Eric Williams declared "one from ten leaves nought" and pulled Trinidad and Tobago out rather than shoulder the financial burden of the smaller Eastern Caribbean islands.
  • Freedom of movement — disputes over unrestricted migration from poorer islands to wealthier Trinidad and Jamaica created constant political friction.

Notably, every one of these fights was about political union, who pays, who votes, who moves. A looser economic union sidesteps most of them, which is why the region eventually built exactly that: CARICOM's single market and the Eastern Caribbean's shared currency show that customs and currency cooperation can survive where federation could not. A small island bargaining alone with a resort developer, or policing its own beaches against sand cartels, is precisely the vulnerability a stronger union was meant to fix.

The takeaway

The sand heist and the Barbuda land fight are the same lesson at two speeds. In Jamaica, a public resource was taken in a single night by people with trucks, connections and impunity. In Barbuda, the transfer is slower and entirely legal: rewrite the land law, issue individual titles, and let debt, foreclosure and market valuations do the rest. In both cases, the fundamental problem is not that communal or public property "doesn't work", it is that communal property is only as strong as the institutions defending it. Barbuda's commons survived hurricanes for a century; whether it survives development capital will depend on courts like the Privy Council, and on whether anyone is willing to try the hybrid models that let an island welcome investment without selling the ground beneath it.

Key Terms

Communal land tenure — a system where land is owned collectively by a community rather than by individuals. Members hold rights to use and occupy land, but cannot sell it to outsiders, keeping the asset inside the community across generations.

Freehold (fee simple) — the fullest form of private land ownership: indefinite, inheritable, and freely sellable or mortgageable by the individual owner.

Leasehold — the right to use land for a fixed period (often 50–99 years) while someone else keeps the underlying title. Long leases can be traded and mortgaged almost like freeholds, then revert to the owner when they expire.

Collateral — an asset pledged to secure a loan, which the lender can seize if the borrower defaults. Land titles are the classic example — which is exactly why titling communal land is double-edged.

Littoral drift (longshore drift) — the natural transport of sand along a coastline by waves and currents. It is why beaches constantly gain and lose sand, and why dumped sand rarely stays put.

Locus standi — the legal right to bring a case before a court. Without standing, even a citizen directly affected by a government decision cannot challenge it.

Tragedy of the commons — the idea that shared resources get overused because no individual bears the full cost of exploiting them. Barbuda is a striking counter-example: a commons governed successfully by community rules for over a century.
