The Gamble of Inflating Away Debt: Can the US Make Its Debt Disappear?

By Gautam Singh · Economics Blog · Inspired by NPR’s Planet Money coverage

When a government owes trillions of dollars, inflation can look like a tempting escape route. Let prices rise, let nominal incomes and GDP catch up, and yesterday’s debts become smaller in real terms. But investors can see it coming and make the next round of borrowing more expensive.

The idea behind inflating away debt is straightforward. If the government has promised to repay a bond with fixed dollar payments, unexpected inflation makes those future dollars worth less. The Treasury can repay the same number of dollars, but those dollars buy less than investors originally expected. Some of the cost is effectively shifted from the government to bondholders.

That does not mean inflation erases the debt, or that every bond is affected equally. The real question is how much debt is exposed, who owns it, and what happens when investors and the Federal Reserve respond.

1. How much debt can actually be eroded?

The first distinction is between conventional Treasury securities and Treasury Inflation Protected Securities, usually known as TIPS. Conventional fixed rate bonds promise dollar payments that do not automatically rise with consumer prices. TIPS adjust their principal with the Consumer Price Index, protecting investors from much of the inflation risk.

TIPS make up only a small share of marketable US Treasury debt, roughly 7%, depending on the date and the debt measure used. Most marketable debt is therefore not directly indexed to inflation.

The maturity structure matters too. The average maturity of US marketable debt is around six years. The government does not refinance the entire stock overnight. Some securities mature soon, while others lock in their existing terms for years. Unexpected inflation can reduce the real value of outstanding fixed rate debt, but the benefit arrives unevenly over time.

When old bonds mature, the Treasury must issue new ones. If investors expect inflation to remain higher, they will demand higher nominal yields to compensate. The government may gain from existing debt while paying more to finance new debt.

The limit: Inflation can reduce the real burden of existing fixed rate debt, especially when it is unexpected. It cannot reliably make future borrowing cheaper if markets anticipate the policy.

2. Who pays for the inflation?

Inflation redistributes purchasing power. The government can benefit when the real value of its nominal liabilities falls, while investors receive repayments that buy less than they expected.

Foreign investors held approximately 30% of publicly held US federal debt in recent estimates. They include overseas private investors and foreign governments. Unexpected US inflation can reduce the real purchasing power of their dollar denominated Treasury holdings. Their outcome in their own currency also depends on exchange rates.

Domestic investors and institutions also hold substantial Treasury debt, including mutual funds, banks, pension funds, households and the Federal Reserve. The effect differs across groups:

  • Households: People holding conventional bonds or bond funds can lose purchasing power when inflation exceeds what they expected. Cash savings can also lose real value.
  • Pension funds: Funds holding nominal bonds may see the real value of those assets fall. Inflation can also increase living costs and some future pension obligations.
  • Foreign holders: They may bear part of the loss on nominal dollar claims, although currency changes can amplify or offset it.
  • TIPS holders: Their principal adjusts with the Consumer Price Index, providing greater protection against inflation than conventional Treasuries.

It would be misleading to say that foreign investors bear exactly 30% of the cost. The distribution depends on the securities affected, their maturity, whether inflation was anticipated and who ultimately owns the claims through pension and investment funds.

3. The Federal Reserve’s independence problem

The government may prefer lower borrowing costs and could benefit in the short run from unexpected inflation. The Federal Reserve, however, is responsible for pursuing price stability and maximum employment. If political pressure pushes the Fed to keep rates too low while inflation is rising, the two objectives can collide.

Economists call the more serious version of this conflict fiscal dominance. Monetary policy becomes constrained by government financing needs. Instead of setting interest rates mainly to stabilise inflation and employment, the central bank faces pressure to accommodate the budget.

Credibility is crucial. If investors believe the Fed will bring inflation under control, long term inflation expectations can remain relatively stable. If they begin to doubt that commitment, they may demand a larger inflation premium on long term bonds. Yields rise, and the government’s future interest bill grows.

That is the paradox: a government trying to reduce its debt burden through inflation could undermine the confidence that allows it to borrow at reasonable rates. Fiscal dominance is a risk, not an automatic outcome, but it becomes more concerning when political pressure weakens confidence in the central bank’s independence.

4. Is real growth a better route than inflation?

A more durable way to make debt manageable is for the economy to grow faster than the real interest rate the government pays on its debt. The key comparison is between r, the real interest rate, and g, real GDP growth.

Change in debt to GDP ≈ (r − g) × existing debt ratio − primary surplus

The primary surplus is government revenue minus non interest spending, expressed as a share of GDP. If growth exceeds the real interest rate, existing debt tends to become smaller relative to the economy, provided the primary deficit is not large enough to overwhelm that advantage.

Consider a simplified example in which public debt begins at 100% of GDP and the primary budget is balanced:

Scenario Real interest rate Real growth Approximate effect
A: borrowing costs exceed growth 3% 2% Debt ratio rises by about 1 percentage point
B: growth exceeds borrowing costs 2% 3% Debt ratio falls by about 1 percentage point

This is an illustration, not a forecast. Actual debt dynamics also depend on the primary budget balance, the effective interest rate on existing debt and other economic adjustments.

Inflation can lift nominal GDP and reduce the ratio of nominal debt to GDP, but its effect on existing debt is strongest when inflation is unexpected. Productivity growth, a larger workforce and sound public finances can improve debt sustainability without relying on a surprise transfer from savers to the government.

5. Is a 5% ten year Treasury yield really high?

A nominal yield is not the same thing as an expected inflation rate. It compensates investors for the time value of money, expected inflation, real returns and the risks of holding a long term bond.

In mid October 2026, the US ten year Treasury yield was around 5.1%. The ten year TIPS real yield was roughly 2.8%, implying a breakeven inflation rate of about 2.3% to 2.4%.

Measure Approximate rate What it tells us
Nominal ten year Treasury 5.1% The quoted yield on a conventional Treasury bond
Ten year TIPS real yield 2.8% The inflation adjusted yield implied by TIPS pricing
Breakeven inflation 2.3% to 2.4% The difference between nominal and TIPS yields

That breakeven rate suggests bond markets were not pricing in runaway inflation over the next decade. It was closer to the Federal Reserve’s 2% longer run target than to an expectation of persistently extreme inflation.

However, breakeven inflation is not a pure forecast. It also reflects inflation risk premiums and differences in the liquidity of conventional Treasuries and TIPS. A 5% nominal yield can coexist with moderate expected inflation if investors demand a high real return or compensation for other risks.

The takeaway

Inflating away debt is possible in a narrow sense: unexpected inflation reduces the real value of outstanding fixed rate government liabilities. But it is a gamble because the gain depends on investors being caught by surprise, while government borrowing needs continue long after the initial inflation shock.

The costs are distributed across bondholders at home and abroad. The strategy can also put pressure on the Federal Reserve’s independence and risk pushing up the yields demanded on new debt. By contrast, sustainable fiscal policy, productivity enhancing growth and manageable real borrowing costs offer a more durable path to reducing the debt burden.

The question is not simply whether the US can inflate away some debt. It is whether it could do so without paying more later through higher interest costs, weaker confidence or a less stable monetary system.

Key terms

TIPS: Treasury Inflation Protected Securities, whose principal adjusts with the US Consumer Price Index.

Breakeven inflation: The difference between the yield on a conventional Treasury bond and a comparable TIPS yield. It is a market based measure of inflation compensation.

Fiscal dominance: A situation in which government financing needs constrain the central bank’s ability to set monetary policy.

Real interest rate: The interest rate after accounting for inflation.

Primary balance: Government revenue minus spending excluding interest payments on existing debt.
